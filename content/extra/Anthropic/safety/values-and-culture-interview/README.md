# The Seven Values and How to Use Them in the Culture Round

## Problem

Read the notes first, then close them and answer the questions. This is the last question in the safety-philosophy review, and its job is to connect the previous six to the actual interview. The full set of culture-round questions is on the [Culture and AI Safety round](#/anthropic/behavioral/culture-ai-safety) page and is not repeated here; this page only practices the kinds of follow-up that page does not cover.

**The seven values** (original wording from the website, checked 2026-11-17):

| Value | What it means | A line worth remembering |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Act for the global good | Decide with humanity's long-term overall interest in mind | “We strive to make decisions that maximize positive outcomes for humanity in the long run.” |
| Hold light and shade | See both the benefits and the dangers of AI, and look away from neither | “We need shade to understand and protect against the potential for bad outcomes. We need light to realize the good outcomes.” |
| Be good to our users | Be good to users, with a very broad definition of “user” | “Users are our customers, policy-makers, Ants, and anyone impacted by the technology we build or the actions we take.” |
| Ignite a race to the top on safety | Push the rest of the industry to compete upward on safety | “We want to constantly set the industry bar for AI safety and security and drive others to do the same.” |
| Do the simple thing that works | Use the simplest approach that works; value impact over how sophisticated the method is | “We don't invent a spaceship if all we need is a bicycle.” |
| Be helpful, honest, and harmless | A high-trust, low-ego way of working together; communicate directly and kindly | “Anthropic is a high-trust, low-ego organization.” |
| Put the mission first | The mission is the final arbiter | “When it comes to our mission, none of us are bystanders.” |

“Ants” is what Anthropic employees call themselves.

**What the culture round looks for.** Not whether you can recite the table above. The interviewer wants to know three things: whether you have genuinely thought about the risks of AI and about how this company approaches them; whether you hold views of your own that you can justify, including criticism; and whether your past behavior is consistent with these values. So ideally each value should map to a concrete experience of your own.

**Four common ways to lose points** (from the advice in the conversation log):

1. **Reciting.** Repeating the website's wording with no examples or judgment of your own.
2. **Going to extremes.** Either doom or “the risks are all hype”; both show you have not seriously weighed the trade-offs.
3. **Performing loyalty.** Endorsing every decision the company has made. The company itself openly admits some of its decisions are contested.
4. **Faking certainty.** Forcing a conclusion on something you don't know. “I'm not sure; here is how I would figure it out” is better.

**Primary sources worth reading before the interview** (in order of importance): [Core Views on AI Safety](https://www.anthropic.com/news/core-views-on-ai-safety); the [RSP page](https://www.anthropic.com/responsible-scaling-policy) and the current version; [Claude's constitution](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution); Dario's two long essays, [Machines of Loving Grace](https://www.darioamodei.com/essay/machines-of-loving-grace) and [The Adolescence of Technology](https://www.darioamodei.com/essay/the-adolescence-of-technology); and research relevant to your role, such as interpretability, [agentic misalignment](https://www.anthropic.com/research/agentic-misalignment), and [alignment faking](https://www.anthropic.com/research/alignment-faking).

For the actual interview format, rely on the [interview process](#/anthropic/process) page and what your recruiter tells you.

**Q1.** Make decisions for the long-term good of all of humanity; face both the benefits and the dangers of AI; be accountable to everyone affected; push the industry to compete upward on safety; use the simplest approach that works; help each other, be honest, do no harm, with high trust and low ego; put the mission first.

**Q2.** It means looking at both sides: looking only at the dangers (shade) misses the good AI can bring, and looking only at the benefits (light) ignores possible bad outcomes; the company asks you to hold both at once. “Users” are not just paying customers; they also include policy-makers, the company's own employees, and anyone affected by the company's technology or actions.

**Q3.** Reciting the website; going to extremes (doom, or not taking it seriously at all); performing loyalty; faking certainty.

**Q4.** There is no standard answer. The question tests whether you have thought independently and whether you are willing to say so. How to prepare:

1. Pick a view you actually hold and can justify. It doesn't need to be startling, but it must be your own.
2. Explain what most people think, why you disagree, and what experience or evidence you are relying on.
3. Say what would change your mind.

Directions you could draw from your background (only examples; use what you actually believe): the gap between evaluation metrics for vision and video models and their real quality; whether the evidence behind claims like “the model understands the physical world” is sufficient; whether safety issues in multimodal models are being overshadowed by the discussion around language models; how academia's publishing culture affects reproducibility.

Answers that don't pass: a view that in fact everyone agrees with; or a view picked to seem distinctive that you can't actually defend.

**Q5.** There is no standard answer. This question pulls together the previous six; use the views you formed there. Possible directions:

- Version 3 of the RSP dropped the pause commitment, which shows voluntary commitments change under pressure;
- Transparency relies mostly on self-reporting, with too little external verification;
- The company's claims about risk point in the same direction as its commercial interests;
- The governance structure has not yet been through a real stress test;
- Whether “put the mission first” can hold up as headcount and revenue grow.

Structure: state the doubt → give the company's strongest response → give your current judgment and what you will keep watching → explain why you still want to join despite the doubt. Don't skip the last step.

**Q6.** This question comes from a third-party compilation of interview reports; the original post was not checked. There is no standard answer. You can answer from two angles, either one or both:

- **Hardest for the company.** For example, “Put the mission first”: the bigger the company and the stronger the revenue pressure, the harder it is to keep the mission as the real final arbiter. Or “Ignite a race to the top”: it depends on others following, which the company does not control.
- **Hardest for you personally.** For example, “Do the simple thing that works”: researchers tend to favor clever methods. Or the direct communication in “Be helpful, honest, and harmless”: telling a senior colleague you disagree is not easy. Describe a specific time you got this wrong, and what you changed afterwards.

Choosing the value that is hardest for you personally is usually more convincing, because it requires self-awareness.

**Q7.** There is no standard answer, but this is worth preparing in concrete detail, because it is where your expertise connects to the company's mission. The conversation log suggests four directions, and for each you should be able to go into technical detail:

1. **Multimodal interpretability.** Most existing interpretability work is done on language models. What the internal representations of vision and video models look like, and whether the same methods can find interpretable features in them, is where your experience is directly useful.
2. **Deepfakes and content provenance.** You know how far generative models can go, where detection methods fail, and how much of a watermark survives compression and re-capture. This is a very concrete piece of the “used to cause harm” category of risk.
3. **Safety evaluation of world models and robot policies.** Once models start acting in the physical world, how do you evaluate in simulation that they won't do anything dangerous, and do the evaluation results transfer to the real environment?
4. **Visual prompt injection.** Text or patterns hidden in images or video can become instructions to an agent. This is a real attack surface for multimodal agents.

When you answer, ground it in specific work you have done: “In project X I ran into phenomenon Y; the safety problem it corresponds to is Z, and here is how I would study it.” Don't just list directions.

**Q8.** This question comes from a third-party compilation of interview reports; the original post was not checked. The corresponding value is “Be good to our users.” Answer in two layers:

- **Principles.** Collect only the data you need; grant access on least privilege; encrypt and de-identify sensitive data; set clear retention periods and delete on expiry; log access so it can be audited; notify affected people promptly when something goes wrong.
- **Your experience.** Give an example of data you actually handled: how you dealt with faces or personal information in a dataset, how you confirmed whether user data could be used for training, and whether there was a time you dropped or changed an approach because of privacy concerns.

If you have never handled user data directly, say so honestly, then talk about similar issues you have run into with research datasets (consent, faces, copyright).
