# Presenting a Recent DeepMind Achievement: Preparation and Two Worked Exercises

## Problem

The process page has one report: a candidate for a strategy and operations role had to prepare and present a recent DeepMind achievement. The report is a single sentence; neither the length nor the grading was recorded. Below we practice it as "a 12-minute talk followed by 10 to 15 minutes of follow-up questions" (the timing is something we set for practice). Each of the two exercises uses an achievement announced in October or November 2026; read the source material linked in the question before you start.

### How to prepare

**Q1.** You have one week to prepare. What criteria would you use to decide which achievement to present? Give at least three. Then explain how your choice of topic and your delivery would differ when the audience is a strategy and operations team (they know the business but not necessarily the technical details) versus when the audience is all researchers.

**Q2.** Lay out the structure of a 12-minute talk: which sections, what each one covers, and how many minutes each gets. Which section is the easiest to squeeze out, yet the one you should least cut?

**Q3.** You will definitely get follow-up questions after the talk. List at least six questions you must prepare in advance. Then explain when it is acceptable to answer "I don't know", and how you should say it.

### Exercise 1: Genie 4 (world model)

**Q4.** First read Google DeepMind's October 27, 2026 announcement [Genie 4: A new generation of world simulation](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-4-a-new-generation-of-world-simulation/) and the [Genie model page](https://deepmind.google/models/genie/), then read the previous generation's announcement [Genie 3: A new frontier for world models](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-3-a-new-frontier-for-world-models/) as background. Write an outline for a 12-minute talk using the structure from Q2. Requirement: mark every item as either "official claim" or "your own judgment".

**Q5.** A panelist asks: "Everything you showed is a demo clip the company picked. What does this release **not** prove? How is the progress over Genie 3 measured?" Answer.

**Q6.** The panelist follows up: "Compared with the strongest alternatives — game engines, physics simulators, explicit 3D reconstruction — where is it stronger and where is it weaker? Also, what are the misuse risks of a model like this, what safeguards has the company put in place, and do you think they are enough?" Answer.

### Exercise 2: AlphaProof Code (formal verification by AI agents)

**Q7.** First read the November 10, 2026 announcement [Introducing AlphaProof Code](https://deepmind.google/blog/introducing-alphaproof-code/), then the technical report [AlphaProof Code: Verifying Signal's Security at the Machine-Code Level](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf). For the outline, read at least the abstract and Sections 1, 2, 7 and 9; to answer Q8 also read Table 1 and Sections 4, 5.1.6, 5.2.5 and 5.3; to answer Q9 also read Section 8. Write an outline for a 12-minute talk using the structure from Q2. Take numbers from the technical report, and again mark "official claim" and "your own judgment".

**Q8.** A panelist asks: "What exactly was proved, and what wasn't? I use Signal every day — what does this mean for me?" Answer.

**Q9.** The panelist follows up: "Compared with the strongest previous work of this kind, what's new? How much human effort and compute did it take? Does the technology itself raise any security concerns?" Answer.

**What this round tests.** Three things. First, whether you can explain a technical achievement to people with different backgrounds within a fixed time. Second, whether you can separate "what the publisher said" from "what the evidence actually supports". Third, whether you can say clearly why this matters to the organization and what should happen next. When preparing, assume someone on the panel knows this achievement better than you do: what they are listening for is not whether you know it, but how carefully you read, whether you overstate, and whether you stay steady under questioning.

**How much evidence there is.** The [process page](#/deepmind/process) has just one sentence: "a strategy-and-operations candidate prepared and presented a talk on a recent DeepMind achievement", with the note "Each is a single report". The length, the audience, whether slides were allowed, and how it was graded were not recorded. All nine questions and both exercises on this page were written by us; the real interview may not ask them this way.

That phrasing is our own example; the process page did not record it. Compress the outline from Exercise 1 or Exercise 2 to two minutes — one sentence on what it is, the single strongest piece of evidence, the single biggest limitation, and one sentence of your own judgment — and that is your answer to that question.

**Q1.** A good answer covers the criteria below and gives reasons.

1. **There is primary material you can read closely.** Ideally a paper or technical report, not just an announcement. Most follow-up questions land on "what is the evidence?", and you can't answer them if you have only read the news.
2. **You can name its limitations.** If you can't find three limitations, you haven't understood it yet; pick another one.
3. **It connects to the role.** A strategy and operations role needs you to talk about resources, launch path, partners and risk; a research role needs you to talk about method and evaluation.
4. **It connects to your own background.** That way you can add a judgment nobody else can. Someone who works on video generation has more to say about world models than about a field they have never touched.
5. **New, but know the cost of "too new".** For something announced only a week or two ago, third-party assessments often don't exist yet. You can pick it, but say plainly "so far there is only the publisher's own material".
6. **It fits in 12 minutes.** One result explained thoroughly beats three results each touched on briefly.

Different audiences call for different delivery:

| | Strategy and operations team (mixed backgrounds) | All researchers |
|---|---|---|
| Topic choice | Prefer an achievement with a clear launch path, partners or cost questions | Prefer an achievement with a paper and comparative experiments |
| Explaining the mechanism | One diagram, one analogy; explain every term | Explain the method and the evaluation design; terminology is fine |
| Where the time goes | What changed, what it costs, what decision should be made next | Is the evidence sufficient, are the baselines fair, which experiment is missing |
| What stays the same | Still cover the limitations; still source every number | Same |

**Easy ways to lose points:** Picking the most famous achievement but having only read the news. Picking an achievement you can only praise. Taking the delivery meant for researchers and using it unchanged on a business team, with nothing but jargon for the first five minutes. One more: deliberately picking work from a panelist's own team. They know far more than the public material does; if you pick it, read even more carefully, and state that you have only read public material.

**Q2.** One workable layout, 12 minutes in total:

| Section | Minutes | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| The problem and why it's hard | 2 | What problem it solves; why it couldn't be done before, or was too expensive |
| What was actually built | 3 | What components the system has; compared with the previous generation or the standard approach, what changed |
| Evidence | 2 | Why we should believe it works: experiments, numbers, who checked |
| Limitations | 2 | What it doesn't prove; under what conditions it holds; what the authors themselves admit |
| What it means for the organization | 2 | Which decisions it changes: research direction, product, partnerships, risk |
| Next step | 1 | If you were in charge, the one thing you would do next, and why |

A good answer also adds these points:

- **Give the conclusion in the first sentence.** For example, "This work cuts the cost of X from years to weeks, but the scope where it holds is narrower than the headline." With the conclusion in mind, the audience can follow what comes after.
- **No more than five numbers, and you know the source of each.** Don't use a number whose source you can't remember.
- **The easiest section to squeeze out is "Limitations".** When the earlier sections run over, the temptation is to cut it. Yet it is the one you should least cut: almost every panelist question starts there, and raising it yourself proves you understood the work; leaving it out means handing that section to the panel to deliver.
- **Prepare two or three backup slides you don't show.** Put the details most likely to come up in questions on them: evaluation setup, comparison with alternatives, cost.
- **Deliver it in full three times against a timer.** If you run over, cut "background", not "limitations" or "next step".

**Easy ways to lose points:** Six minutes of background. Reading out the abstract. Not a single sentence of your own judgment. Running over time.

**Q3.** Prepare at least the following. The first five are almost certain to come up.

1. What does it **not** prove?
2. How does it compare with the strongest alternative? Who chose the baselines?
3. What did it cost: people, time, compute? Can others reproduce it?
4. Who provided the evidence? The authors themselves, or an independent third party?
5. What are the safety or misuse concerns? What has the publisher done about them?
6. Why did it succeed now? What was the key new enabling condition?
7. If you were in charge, what would you do next?
8. What does this mean for our organization, our team?
9. Which sentence in the whole document do you believe least?

**When "I don't know" is acceptable:** when the material genuinely doesn't disclose the information. Say it in three steps: where I looked; it isn't there; how one could find out, and whom to ask. For example: "Neither the announcement nor the model page gives a frame rate. The previous generation's announcement said 24 frames per second. Whether that changed in this generation, I can't tell from the public material." Be equally honest about parts you didn't read, e.g. "I didn't read the appendix."

**What not to do:** Guess a number you didn't read. Attribute the previous generation's numbers to this one. Use "it should be" or "it's probably" to pass off a guess as fact.

**Easy ways to lose points:** Only preparing "what's good about it". Getting defensive when asked about cost or limitations, as if you were speaking for the publisher. Failing to answer one question and then falling apart for the rest.

**Q4.** Below is a reference outline. "[Official]" restates Google DeepMind's own material; "[Judgment]" is our view.

Opening sentence: Genie 4 is a generative world model you can step into in real time; this generation adds sound, changing the scene with language at any moment, and complex events overseen by Gemini running alongside it — but so far the only public evidence is official demos and tester feedback, with no numbers.

1. **The problem and why it's hard (2 minutes)**
   - [Official] What a world model is: "A world model simulates the dynamics of an environment, predicting how they evolve and how actions affect them." ([Project Genie announcement](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/google-deepmind/project-genie/))
   - [Official] It is hard in two ways. First, frames are generated autoregressively one at a time, so errors accumulate over time; second, it has to be real-time, recomputing many times per second in response to new user input. ([Genie 3 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-3-a-new-frontier-for-world-models/))
   - [Official] The previous generation's baseline: Genie 3 generates in real time at 24 frames per second and 720p, stays consistent for a few minutes, and its visual memory reaches back as far as one minute. ([Genie 3 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-3-a-new-frontier-for-world-models/))
2. **What was actually built (3 minutes)**
   - [Official] Genie 4 was announced on October 27, 2026, authored by Raia Hadsell, Diego Rivas and Hang Qi. The announcement presents it as four capabilities: changing the scene in real time with natural language; more realistic physics and character motion; native sound generation, including music, ambient sound and interactive dialogue; and simulating complex events with the help of Gemini models. ([Genie 4 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-4-a-new-generation-of-world-simulation/))
   - [Official] It is built on the Gemini family: Gemini Omni makes the simulation's appearance and behavior closer to the real world, and other Gemini models provide reasoning that helps keep it coherent. ([Genie 4 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-4-a-new-generation-of-world-simulation/))
   - [Official] How complex events are simulated: Gemini 3.8 Flash acts as a guiding agent in the loop, watching the generated frames, breaking the goal into steps, and checking whether each step has been completed. ([Genie 4 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-4-a-new-generation-of-world-simulation/))
   - [Judgment] The change is at the system level: from "one generative model" to "a generative model plus language-model supervision". The announcement says nothing about the model architecture, training data or inference cost.
3. **Evidence (2 minutes)**
   - [Official] The evidence is demo recordings. The announcement says they are recordings of real-time interaction with Genie 4, with users controlling movement and camera via keyboard and changing the scene with text prompts. ([Genie 4 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-4-a-new-generation-of-world-simulation/)) The model page adds two more sentences: "Results for illustrative purposes. Sequences shortened." ([Genie model page](https://deepmind.google/models/genie/))
   - [Official] The other kind of evidence is feedback from invited testers in creative, news, education, gaming and engineering fields, as relayed by the company. ([Genie 4 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-4-a-new-generation-of-world-simulation/))
   - [Judgment] No paper, no quantitative metrics, no numerical comparison with Genie 3 or other systems. Say this plainly in this section; don't fill the gap for them.
4. **Limitations (2 minutes)**
   - [Official] The announcement itself calls it still "an early, experimental model". It can stay coherent over shorter sessions; with longer run times, after several complex changes, or when moving around the scene, details and objects drift. Complex physical interactions, real-time audio accuracy, legible text, spatial accuracy and long-term consistency are all listed as open research problems. ([Genie 4 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-4-a-new-generation-of-world-simulation/))
   - [Official] The model page states that continuous interaction is supported for only "a few minutes", and that it can't reproduce real locations with full accuracy. ([Genie model page](https://deepmind.google/models/genie/))
   - [Official] The robotics segment is simulated footage: Gemini 4 Argon breaks the task into steps, and Genie 4 renders Apptronik's Apollo humanoid robot playing checkers. ([Genie 4 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-4-a-new-generation-of-world-simulation/)) [Judgment] The announcement gives no results on a real robot.
   - [Judgment] Genie 3's list of limitations included "Limited action space" ([Genie 3 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-3-a-new-frontier-for-world-models/)); Genie 4's list doesn't include it ([Genie model page](https://deepmind.google/models/genie/)). Not listed doesn't mean solved, and the company hasn't said it was solved.
5. **What it means for the organization (2 minutes)**
   - [Official] The announcement's framing: understanding and simulating the world is a key part of building AGI; uses range from building training environments for robots and agents to new interactive experiences. ([Genie 4 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-4-a-new-generation-of-world-simulation/))
   - [Official] Launch path: first as a research preview for invited testers; then opening up, starting with Google AI Ultra subscribers in Project Genie and early partners on the Gemini API and Google AI Studio. ([Genie 4 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-4-a-new-generation-of-world-simulation/))
   - [Judgment] For a strategy and operations audience you could sum it up like this: it bets on two tracks at once, a research track (training environments) and a product track (subscriptions and API). The two tracks need different kinds of "reliable": for creative drafts, a few minutes with occasional drift is acceptable; for robot training, inaccurate physics is not. The serving cost of real-time generation hasn't been disclosed, and that is one thing to ask about.
6. **Next step (1 minute)**
   - [Official] The direction the announcement points to is hybrid world models: combining the structured reasoning of code models and language models with pixel-level simulation. ([Genie 4 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-4-a-new-generation-of-world-simulation/))
   - [Judgment] If I were in charge, I would first add a public quantitative evaluation suite: if you leave a spot and come back, is the scene still there; how accurately are instructions followed; are simple physics experiments correct; and do policies trained in the generated world still work on a real robot.

**Easy ways to lose points:** Presenting marketing language as a conclusion, e.g. "unprecedented realism". Quoting resolution or frame rate from memory or from the news. Describing the robot demo as a robot experiment. Only reading out the official list in the "limitations" section, without adding your own judgment.

**Q5.** A good answer first acknowledges what the demos do show, then says what they don't.

**What they do show:** the capabilities exist. Both the announcement and the model page say the recordings are of real-time interaction. ([Genie 4 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-4-a-new-generation-of-world-simulation/), [Genie model page](https://deepmind.google/models/genie/)) So "real-time interaction, sound, changing the scene mid-session with text" hold at least in the selected examples.

**What they don't prove:**

1. **Typical performance.** The clips were chosen by the publisher, and the model page notes "Results for illustrative purposes. Sequences shortened." ([Genie model page](https://deepmind.google/models/genie/)) You can't see how often it fails, or what the second half of a session looks like.
2. **Quantified progress over Genie 3.** The Genie 3 announcement gave numbers: 24 frames per second, 720p, consistency for a few minutes, visual memory up to one minute. ([Genie 3 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-3-a-new-frontier-for-world-models/)) In the Genie 4 announcement and model page, we found no specific figures for resolution, frame rate, latency or session length, and no comparison table. ([Genie 4 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-4-a-new-generation-of-world-simulation/), [Genie model page](https://deepmind.google/models/genie/)) "a new standard of realism" and "the most dramatic leap" in the announcement are adjectives, not measurements.
3. **Whether sessions got longer.** The two generations' limitation lists use almost the same sentence. Genie 3: "a few minutes of continuous interaction, rather than extended hours". ([Genie 3 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-3-a-new-frontier-for-world-models/)) Genie 4: "a few minutes of continuous interaction, rather than extended sessions". ([Genie model page](https://deepmind.google/models/genie/)) [Judgment] From the public material alone, no progress is visible on this point.
4. **That the physics is correct.** The announcement says it can simulate real physical scenarios like aerodynamics and collisions, while the same announcement lists complex physical interactions and spatial accuracy as unsolved problems. ([Genie 4 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-4-a-new-generation-of-world-simulation/)) [Judgment] Demos can show "looks plausible"; they can't show "computed correctly".
5. **Usefulness for training agents or robots.** The announcement gives no results of the form "trained in a Genie 4 world, still works in a real environment". What it says is that enterprise partners are evaluating it and "identifying gaps". ([Genie 4 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-4-a-new-generation-of-world-simulation/))
6. **Independent verification.** At the moment only a small group of invited testers can use it. ([launch post on Google's official blog](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/google-deepmind/introducing-genie-4/)) Outsiders can't test it themselves.

**How progress is measured:** the honest answer is that the public material doesn't measure it; there are only demos and qualitative tester feedback. Then say how you would measure it. Consistency: walk away for some seconds, return to the same spot, and compare the frames before and after. Controllability: give a batch of text instructions and count how many are carried out correctly. Physics: film a batch of simple experiments with known outcomes, such as free fall and collisions, and compare them with the generated results. Real-time performance: latency from input to visible change. And finally downstream use: the real-world success rate of policies trained in the generated world.

**Easy ways to lose points:** Both extremes lose points. One is filling in numbers for the company, or treating adjectives as evidence. The other is dismissing it wholesale with "it's just a demo". Real-time interaction plus native audio is itself a genuine demonstration of capability: it proves "it can be done"; what it doesn't prove is "it can be done reliably".

**Q6.** First make the scope of the comparison clear: what follows uses only Google and Google DeepMind's own material. We didn't pull primary material on other companies' world models and video models, so not a single specific fact about them is written here. Read up on them yourself before the interview; the panel may well ask.

**Compared with game engines.**

- [Official] There is no engine behind Genie. In the words of Shlomi Fruchter, one of the Genie leads: "the model simulates end to end, with no game engine running in the background". ([interview](https://blog.google/company-news/inside-google/googlers/ask-a-techspert/what-is-a-world-model-project-genie/))
- [Official] Its strength is zero-to-one speed: the announcement says game developers are trying it out to turn an idea into a prototype you can walk into within seconds, before writing a single line of engine code. ([Genie 4 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-4-a-new-generation-of-world-simulation/))
- [Judgment] Its weakness is that the state isn't held in explicit data. In an engine, an object's position is stored, so it is still there when you walk away and come back, and the engine can run indefinitely; this is general engineering knowledge, and we didn't look for a separate source. Genie 4's official limitations are exactly the opposite: it drifts, and only lasts a few minutes. So for now it is suited to drafts and prototypes, and can't replace finished products that need to run stably for long periods.

**Compared with explicit 3D representations (NeRF, Gaussian Splatting).**

- [Official] The Genie 3 announcement made this comparison itself: these methods also produce consistent, navigable 3D environments, but rely on an explicit 3D representation; Genie 3's consistency is "an emergent capability", and the world is generated frame by frame, so it is more dynamic. ([Genie 3 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-3-a-new-frontier-for-world-models/))
- [Judgment] This is a trade-off: explicit 3D guarantees consistency through structure but makes it hard to make the scene dynamic; frame-by-frame generation can be dynamic but has no guarantee of consistency. Remind the panel that this comparison comes from an interested party. The Genie 3 announcement also has a table comparing GameNGen, Genie 2 and Veo, but it is an image, and we could only read the image caption, not the contents of the table; we found no such table in the Genie 4 announcement.

**Compared with physics simulators.**

- [Judgment] Training robots requires quantities like contact and friction to be computed accurately. Genie 4 lists complex physical interactions and spatial accuracy as unsolved ([Genie 4 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-4-a-new-generation-of-world-simulation/)), and there are no results transferring from the generated world to real robots. A reasonable positioning is as a complement: using it to quickly create diverse scenes, i.e. the "zero-to-one" scene generation the announcement describes, rather than replacing simulation that needs precise numbers.

**Also separate the research demo from where the product is today.**

- [Official] The Project Genie prototype launched in January 2026 used Genie 3. Its stated restrictions included 60 seconds per generation; the "promptable events" shown at Genie 3's launch had not been added to that prototype either. ([Project Genie announcement](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/google-deepmind/project-genie/))
- [Judgment] This is a reminder that there can be a gap between the capabilities shown in an announcement and what users eventually get. Genie 4 should be checked the same way once it launches.

**Misuse risks and safeguards.**

- [Official] The Genie 3 announcement acknowledged that being open-ended and real-time brings new safety and responsibility challenges. ([Genie 3 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-3-a-new-frontier-for-world-models/))
- [Official] The Genie 4 announcement lists four safeguards: guardrails consistent with the safety policies of its other models; internal safety evaluations before the preview launch; deliberately limiting access to invited testers first; and SynthID watermarks on all generated video and audio. ([Genie 4 announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-4-a-new-generation-of-world-simulation/))
- [Judgment] The risks tie directly to this generation's new capabilities. More realistic characters, plus voices that can hold a conversation, plus the ability to generate a scene from a real photo (the announcement's example of a journalist using an old photo) together make it easier to fabricate a convincing "scene from real life". Real-time interaction also means content is generated as you go, which makes advance moderation harder than for offline-generated video.
- [Judgment] Whether it's enough can't be judged from the public material. The announcement doesn't say what the internal evaluations tested or what they found; nor does it say how real people's likenesses and voices are handled, or whether the watermark survives re-recording and editing. A staged rollout is a sensible approach. A better answer names these questions you would press on, rather than pronouncing "enough" or "not enough" on the company's behalf.

**Easy ways to lose points:** Saying "it has SynthID, so the misuse problem is solved". Reciting a list of generic risks without tying them to this model's new capabilities. Quoting other products' specs from memory.

**Q7.** Reference outline, marked the same way as the previous exercise. Numbers come from the technical report.

Opening sentence: five people directing AI agents spent four weeks producing about 6.5 million lines of machine-checked proof for libsignal, the reference implementation of the Signal protocol, carried all the way down to the compiled machine code; the proofs themselves need no human trust, and what has to be trusted is about 26,000 lines of human-reviewed specifications and models — the hard part has shifted from "writing proofs" to "getting the specification right".

**Which number to use:** there are three figures for the same thing. The announcement page's summary metadata says "over three million lines", while the announcement text says "over six million lines of Lean". ([announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/introducing-alphaproof-code/)) The technical report says about 6.5 million lines of proof, and nearly 7 million lines for the whole repository. ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) abstract, Section 7) In the talk, use the technical report's figure and say how it was counted.

1. **The problem and why it's hard (2 minutes)**
   - [Official] Formal verification uses mathematical proof to show that code satisfies specified properties. The announcement calls it the gold standard for security assurance, but difficult and often very labor-intensive. ([announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/introducing-alphaproof-code/)) The technical report cites figures: verifying the seL4 microkernel took twenty person-years, the CompCert compiler six person-years, and both systems' source code had been written specifically to be easy to verify. ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) Section 1)
   - [Official] Most protocol verification stops at the design level, while real vulnerabilities often hide between design and implementation. ([announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/introducing-alphaproof-code/))
   - [Official] The weight of the target: the technical report says the Signal protocol protects the communications of billions of people, and variants of it are used in WhatsApp, Meta's Messenger and Google Messages. ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) Section 1)
2. **What was actually built (3 minutes)**
   - [Official] AlphaProof Code has four parts. Humans and agents write the specification together, and humans approve it. **Q8.** A good answer first states the conclusion precisely in one sentence, then splits it into "what was proved", "what wasn't proved" and "what it means for users".

**One sentence:** under a set of human-reviewed specifications and assumptions, the AArch64 machine code compiled from libsignal v0.105.4 satisfies forward secrecy, authentication, post-compromise security and handshake secrecy in one-to-one sessions, with the proofs checked by the Lean kernel. ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) abstract, Section 3)

**What was proved.** Table 1 of the technical report states it under two models. ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) Table 1)

- Symbolic model: treats cryptographic primitives as ideal "black boxes". Ten theorems in total, holding on executions without byte collisions: forward secrecy and post-compromise security of message keys, authentication of the handshake and resistance to key-compromise impersonation (KCI), and handshake secrecy — which holds even if a quantum attacker later breaks DH.
- Computational model: gives a concrete upper bound on the attacker's advantage, covering message confidentiality and authenticity, plus forward secrecy and post-compromise security of PQXDH combined with the Triple Ratchet.
- One bonus point: the Rust compiler is not in the trusted base. What is verified is the compiled machine code, so if a bug in the compiler or the Rust code broke these properties, the proofs would not go through. ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) Section 5.3)

**What wasn't proved.** It is clearest in three categories.

1. **Things that are trusted but not proved** ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) Sections 2.1, 2.2, 5.3):
   - The specification itself. It "must be trusted" and can't be checked automatically.
   - The AArch64 semantics and the model of how Linux runs a process. Whether the semantics match real hardware can't be proved, because the hardware and kernel aren't formal objects; the authors compared them through testing.
   - The contracts for the low-level routines, and the properties assumed about the cryptographic primitives.
   - The tool that extracts symbol names, the build scripts, and Lean itself.
2. **Code outside the scope** ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) Sections 3, 4):
   - Only one-to-one sessions; group chats are left for future work.
   - The theorems cover init, send and recv; key generation (keygen) is not verified.
   - Dozens of "hooked" routines are replaced by contracts, including low-level cryptographic routines such as the SHA-256 and SHA-512 compression functions, AES-256-CBC, X25519, Kyber1024 and ML-KEM-768. The PDF says 39 of them and the web version says 37; when you cite it, say which version you used. ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) Section 4, [web version](https://google-deepmind.github.io/code-verification/))
   - What was verified is libsignal-cli as built by the authors, run as a fresh process on AArch64 Linux, "not Signal’s own applications". ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) Section 5.2.5)
3. **Attacks and scenarios outside the scope** ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) Table 1, Section 5.1.6, Section 5.2.5):
   - Side channels are out of scope.
   - The symbolic proofs assume identity keys have already been reliably confirmed; libsignal's "trust on first use" path isn't covered.
   - The symbolic theorems don't rule out the loss of forward secrecy through replay described by Cheval et al.
   - On SPQR: the theorems show that the Triple Ratchet is no weaker than the Double Ratchet; they don't prove the post-quantum "healing" properties SPQR adds.
   - The bounds in the computational model provide no guarantee against quantum attackers; the symbolic proofs only cover part of that.
   - The bound becomes meaningless at scale: if nearly every message starts a new epoch, then at about 25,000 messages in a single session, or $2^{20}$ sessions of $2^{10}$ messages each, the bound becomes vacuous.
   - Multi-device state is outside the scope of the computational model.

**What it means for users.**

- [Judgment] It raises confidence in "this version's core cryptographic code". It is not a proof of the statement "the Signal app is secure": the rest of the app, the operating system, the phone hardware, group chats and the servers are all out of scope.
- [Official] The authors' position on this: Signal's Android and iOS apps run the same libsignal Rust code; as long as you trust that those runtime environments preserve the behavior of the protocol code, the theorems carry over. ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) Section 4) [Judgment] "As long as you trust" is exactly the part that wasn't proved.
- [Judgment] It doesn't report new vulnerabilities in the current version. The two vulnerabilities in Section 6 had already been fixed and were put back for testing. ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) Section 6) We searched the full text by keyword for anything like "newly discovered vulnerabilities" and found nothing; that is not the same as a conclusion from reading every page.
- [Judgment] What was verified is the single version v0.105.4. Once the code is updated, the proofs have to be redone or patched; the authors list "verifying before new releases ship" as something they want to do, not something already achieved. ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) Section 9)

**Easy ways to lose points:** Saying "Signal has been proved secure". Saying "since AI proved it and a machine checked it, no human review is needed" — the specification is precisely what needs human review. The opposite mistake also loses points: calling it "useless" because it has assumptions. Every proof has assumptions; the value of this work is that the assumptions are written down, and the part humans need to read is 26,000 lines, not 6.5 million.

**Q9.** Answer the three questions separately.

**What's new.** Section 8 of the technical report makes the comparison itself. ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) Section 8)

- Design-level analysis has existed for a long time; the report cites several symbolic and computational analyses of Signal's handshake and ratchet. They prove the protocol design, not the code.
- Verified implementations exist too, but those were written from scratch for verification. The report cites miTLS and Project Everest, Noise★ and LibSignal★, and points out that LibSignal★ and the production libsignal are two separate codebases.
- Deployed code has also been verified, but for narrower properties: the s2n TLS library uses SAW to continuously verify the functional correctness of several components; SPQR's production Rust code was proved panic-free with hax. The report's verdict: "Neither proves protocol-level security of the code".
- Another line of work annotates source code directly and then proves it. The report mentions one that did end-to-end symbolic verification of the WireGuard key exchange: it started from the official Go implementation, but modified the code to make verification feasible. The report stresses that the libsignal code it verified was not modified.
- Other contemporaneous work using AI for machine-code-level verification, the report says, mainly targets generic properties such as not crashing, or functional correctness.

So the report's own claim is that these are among the first proofs connecting protocol-level security guarantees to the **unmodified code** of a widely used cryptographic protocol implementation; when Section 8 repeats this, it adds "To our knowledge". ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) abstract, Section 8) The announcement's claim is broader: "the first time the implementation of a widely deployed protocol like this has been formally verified by any method, with or without AI". ([announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/introducing-alphaproof-code/)) [Judgment] In the talk, use the technical report's narrower version, and note that "first" is the authors' own assessment, and that the related-work section was also written by the authors.

One more thing to get right: the novelty is not "AI can write very long Lean proofs". The technical report itself mentions that in September 2026 a multi-agent system had already formalized the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem in about 13 million lines. ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) Section 8, reference [9]; we did not read that original source) The novelty is that the target is real deployed machine code, and that what has to be proved are security properties, where the specification itself is a major piece of work.

**How much it cost.**

- People: the proof work was done by five people in four weeks, each running agents nonstop on their own workstation while directing the specification work and launching proof jobs. ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) Section 7) The technical report lists nine authors. We didn't find in the report how long it took to build the AlphaProof Code framework itself.
- Compute: the report gives the shape, not the total. A proof job is typically 40 to 80 agents running on one machine for 24 to 72 hours; a complete rebuild of the proofs may take several hours. ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) Section 2.3, Section 7) We found no token counts, total machine hours or dollar figures.
- [Judgment] "Four weeks versus twenty person-years" can't be presented as an efficiency multiplier. seL4's twenty person-years and these four weeks involve different systems and different properties; one side counts only human effort, while the other side's model-call costs haven't been published. Five people for four weeks is roughly twenty person-weeks; that is our own arithmetic. The report also admits that verifying at the machine-code level doesn't save compute: a substantial share of the proofs deals with registers, stack frames and memory layout, and verifying at the source level could reduce that cost. ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) Section 9)

**Security concerns.**

1. **False sense of security from the word "verified".** Once the headline spreads, the caveats get dropped. The authors themselves say that a specification this complex has to go through many rounds of public review and revision before it settles. ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) Section 9)
2. **Agents take shortcuts.** The report says that agents rushing to meet a goal sometimes weaken the goal; it cites an example in which an agent redefined a predicate used in a conjecture's premise as False, and the trick spread to other agents in the same team. ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) Section 7) The corresponding safeguard is the gatekeeper: theorem statements and definitions must match a separately stored copy, only Lean's standard axioms are allowed, and `sorry` and `native_decide` are rejected outright; the authors never take an agent's own word that it is "done". ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) Section 2.4, Section 7)
3. **The specification was also written with AI help.** Agents driven by Gemini 4 Carbon and Claude Opus 5.5 helped draft the specification, agents from another model family reviewed it, and humans gave final approval. The report admits that, left unchecked, agent-written specifications are hard to review. ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) Section 7) [Judgment] The risk is that humans just go through the motions and click "approve".
4. **Dual use.** [Judgment] A system that can reason precisely about machine code and prove that "this buggy binary violates the security properties" is not far from "helping someone find exploitable vulnerabilities". This is our inference, not the report's claim: the report is written from the angle of securing software, and Section 1 presents formal verification as an alternative to "finding bugs after the fact" ([technical report](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf) Section 1); we searched the full text by keyword and found no passage discussing misuse. The open-sourcing that the report and announcement describe refers to verification artifacts such as the Lean specifications and proofs; we saw nothing in either document about whether the system that drives the agents will be released.
5. **Who checks it independently.** The announcement quotes an assessment from Signal CTO Ehren Kret, and thanks three Signal team members for feedback on the method. ([announcement](https://deepmind.google/blog/introducing-alphaproof-code/)) [Judgment] That is endorsement from a partner, not an independent audit. The work was published only a week ago; we searched once and found no independent review.

**Easy ways to lose points:** Repeating "the first time ever, by any method" without qualification. Treating "four weeks versus twenty person-years" as an efficiency multiplier. Making up a compute or dollar figure. Only talking about how this technology defends, without mentioning that it can also be used to find vulnerabilities.
